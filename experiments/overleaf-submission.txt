\begin{abstract}
Supervised vulnerability detection depends on labelled data that is scarce,
noisy, and quickly stale, and cannot flag a weakness unlike anything in its
training set. We ask whether a function's own documentation can serve as the
label instead. A language model that has never seen the code writes
implementations from the specification alone; a second model is shown one
version of the real function at a time, never told which version it is or that
a vulnerability is involved, and asked which defensive steps the
reconstructions perform that this version omits. Whichever version is rated
more exposed is called the vulnerable one. Two choices turned out to matter,
and we arrived at both by measurement rather than by design intuition. The
judge is asked a narrow, non-lexical question instead of a broad similarity
question, since a broad comparison of two implementations is answered
substantially lexically. And it is asked about the \emph{consensus} of five
independent reconstructions instead of one, since a single reconstruction
rarely contains the guard the vulnerable version lacks. On 539 non-duplicate
CVE fix-pairs from a validated Big-Vul-derived corpus, the redesigned
formulation reaches \textbf{66.8\%} pairwise accuracy on the 38\% of pairs it
decides (95\% CI [60.4\%, 73.3\%], $p = 1.6\times10^{-6}$ against a 50\%
chance level), with no supervised signal at any stage. Restricting to pairs
whose two versions have identical line counts, where the obvious length
shortcut carries no information, leaves accuracy undiminished at 68.5\%. We
also report the lexical dependence of LLM code comparison, which constrains
LLM-as-judge designs beyond this task.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Automated software vulnerability detection is dominated by supervised
classifiers that learn to associate code patterns with previously labeled
CVEs \cite{hanif2022vulberta, fu2022linevul}. This works well within the
training distribution, but by construction such a classifier cannot flag
a vulnerability that does not resemble anything it has already seen. This
limitation matters most for exactly the vulnerabilities worth catching
early: novel weakness types, deliberately obfuscated bugs, and backdoors
inserted as part of a supply-chain compromise.

This paper asks a different question: instead of ``does this code
resemble a known vulnerability?'', does this code do something its own
specification never called for? A function's documentation encodes its
intended behavior; the implementation may or may not match that intent,
and a vulnerability is one possible cause of such a mismatch alongside
several benign ones: stylistic variation, incidental refactoring, and
ordinary bugs. Distinguishing security-relevant drift from the rest of
that mismatch, without training on vulnerability labels at all, is the
central technical problem this paper addresses.

The approach operationalizes this as a round-trip consistency check, in
the spirit of round-trip translation for reference-free quality
estimation \cite{moon2020revisiting}: given only a natural-language
specification, independent implementations are regenerated from scratch,
and systematic divergence between the regenerations and the real code is
treated as the detection signal. Concretely (\S\ref{sec:methodology}), a
specification-only \emph{Generator} reconstructs each function five times with
no access to the original code, and a \emph{Judge}, following the
LLM-as-judge paradigm for evaluating open-ended generation
\cite{zheng2023judging}, is shown those reconstructions together with a single
version of the real function, without being told which version it is, and asked
which defensive steps a majority of the reconstructions perform that this
version omits. Ground truth comes from real CVE-fix pairs drawn from the
Big-Vul corpus \cite{fan2020ac}, so that a vulnerable and a fixed
implementation of the same specification are available for every case, and
detection performance can be measured without assuming a base rate.

Neither design choice above was obvious in advance. Our first implementation
put a broad similarity question to the judge against a single reconstruction,
and it carries no signal at all. \S\ref{sec:evolution} reports the diagnosis
that led from there to the method evaluated here, since that diagnosis is
itself a result: an LLM judge asked to compare two implementations is doing
something substantially lexical, while a specification-only reconstruction
shares almost no identifiers with the code it reconstructs.

The design is scoped as a precursor to supervised classification rather than a
replacement for it. We intend the detector to act as a filter that routes
anomalous functions to a downstream classifier, so that expensive,
label-dependent classification is spent only on code already flagged as
inconsistent with its own specification. This paper implements and evaluates
that anomaly-detection stage alone.

Our contributions are: (1) a specification-only regeneration and blind
single-reference LLM-judge pipeline requiring no vulnerability-labeled training
data, no task-specific training component, no external static analyser, and no
CVE knowledge base; (2) a paired evaluation design over real CVE
vulnerable/fixed pairs that measures detection performance without assuming a
base rate, on a corpus validated case-by-case against the raw archive; (3) a
detector reaching 66.8\% pairwise accuracy against a 50\% chance level,
obtained by redesigning the judge's question and drawing five reconstructions
instead of one, with no supervised signal introduced at any stage; and (4) the
finding that an LLM judge's apparent ability to compare two implementations
behaviourally is largely lexical, which constrains LLM-as-judge code-comparison
designs beyond this one.


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Learning-based function-level vulnerability detection}
\label{sec:rw-supervised}

Deep-learning approaches to function-level vulnerability detection (VD) train classifiers that map source code to a binary or CWE-typed verdict. Early sequence models such as VulDeePecker \citep{zou2019mu} classified token-level ``code gadgets'' with a BLSTM. Graph-based methods, exemplified by Devign \citep{zhou2019devign}, encode the abstract syntax tree, control-flow graph, and data-flow graph of a function into a composite program graph and apply a gated graph neural network. Transformer-based approaches subsequently advanced benchmark performance: LineVul fine-tunes a pretrained code transformer and reuses its attention weights for line-level localisation \citep{fu2022linevul}, and VulBERTa pretrains a RoBERTa-style model with a custom C/C++ tokenizer for vulnerability classification \citep{hanif2022vulberta}.

Reported benchmark accuracy of these models overstates deployment performance: classifiers generalise poorly to unseen projects and produce high false-positive rates in practice \citep{chakraborty2021deep}. More strikingly, \citet{risse2024uncovering} show that top ML4VD models, despite $\sim$70\% benchmark accuracy, perform worse than random when required to distinguish vulnerable from patched versions of the same function, attributing the gap to overfitting on features unrelated to vulnerability labels.

\subsection{LLMs as direct vulnerability detectors}
\label{sec:rw-llmvd}

A second line of work replaces the trained classifier with a prompted LLM. Across sixteen pretrained LLMs, five datasets, and twenty-five CWEs, \citet{khare2025understanding} report an average accuracy of $62.8\%$ and F1 of $0.71$, characterising the observed performance as modest. \citet{steenhoek2024comprehensive} report that LLM verdicts are prompt-sensitive and inconsistent across near-duplicate functions, and \citet{ullah2024llms} argue that current LLMs cannot reliably identify or reason about security vulnerabilities.

The shared design prompts the LLM to classify whether a given function is vulnerable. The framework proposed here instead recasts the task as specification consistency: one LLM reimplements the function from its specification and a second, blinded LLM compares the reconstruction against a reference.

\subsection{Vulnerability datasets built from CVE fixes}
\label{sec:rw-datasets}

Function-level VD is trained and evaluated on datasets that pair vulnerable and fixed versions of C/C++ functions mined from CVE-referenced commits. BigVul \citep{fan2020ac} provides approximately $11{,}000$ such pairs across roughly $3{,}700$ CVEs and forms the basis of the corpus used here. The Devign dataset \citep{zhou2019devign} contributes a smaller, hand-labelled collection with narrower project scope, and CVEfixes \citep{bhandari2021cvefixes} extends automated collection to additional languages. DiverseVul \citep{chen2023diversevul} expands the data landscape and documents the duplication and label-noise problems that affect earlier BigVul-family datasets.

PrimeVul \citep{ding2025primevul} introduces stricter deduplication, human-verified labelling, and a pair-mode evaluation in which each model must produce jointly consistent verdicts on the vulnerable and fixed versions of the same function. Under this evaluation, a 7B code model drops from $68.26\%$ F1 on BigVul to $3.09\%$ F1 on PrimeVul \citep{ding2025primevul}. The present evaluation adopts a pair-based framing consistent with this design: each case is a $(f_{\text{vuln}}, f_{\text{fixed}})$ pair, and the primary metric is defined jointly over both sides. The paired datasets are used solely as an evaluation substrate; no CVE labels enter the pipeline.

\subsection{LLM-as-judge and generate-and-verify}
\label{sec:rw-judge}

Using one LLM to score the output of another is an established evaluation primitive: \citet{zheng2023judging} demonstrate strong agreement between LLM judges and human raters on MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena. In the code domain, CodeT \citep{chen2023codet} separates candidate generation from oracle generation, using dual execution agreement to rank solutions. The architecture proposed here retains the generator--verifier separation but substitutes an LLM behavioural judgement for execution-based agreement, since CVE-fix pairs seldom compile in isolation and executing attacker-influenced inputs against real vulnerabilities is not a viable evaluation primitive. The use of two independent judge invocations per case follows the self-consistency motivation of \citet{chen2023universal}.

\subsection{Unsupervised and label-free vulnerability detection}
\label{sec:rw-unsupervised}

A parallel line of research reduces or eliminates the reliance on CVE labels. Because the proposed framework belongs to this class, four representative approaches are examined here.

\paragraph{Self-supervised representation learning} ContraFlow \citep{cheng2022contraflow} pretrains a value-flow path encoder via contrastive learning on unlabelled programs, then fine-tunes a downstream detector on a smaller labelled set. The pretrained representations reduce the labelled data required for downstream fine-tuning, though supervised training on labelled vulnerable/safe pairs remains necessary at the final stage.

\paragraph{Neuro-symbolic LLM-augmented static analysis.} IRIS \citep{li2025iris} couples an LLM with the CodeQL static analyser: the LLM infers project-specific taint specifications and performs contextual reasoning, replacing manual specifications that CodeQL otherwise requires. On CWE-Bench-Java, IRIS with GPT-4 detects 55 of 120 real-world vulnerabilities against CodeQL's 27 and identifies four previously unknown vulnerabilities in mature Java projects.

\paragraph{Retrieval-augmented LLM detection.} Vul-RAG \citep{du2024vul} extracts multi-dimensional vulnerability knowledge from historical CVE fixes into a queryable knowledge base and retrieves relevant knowledge to condition an LLM on each candidate function. The approach requires no model fine-tuning; \citet{du2024vul} report that Vul-RAG detected ten previously-unknown bugs in the recent Linux kernel release, six of which received assigned CVEs.

\paragraph{Unsupervised anomaly detection over pretrained embeddings.} HYDRA \citep{farhad2026hydra} is the closest architectural cousin to the framework proposed here. It encodes patched functions with GraphCodeBERT, concatenates a five-dimensional handcrafted heuristic vector, projects the concatenation into a latent space via a variational autoencoder, and applies K-means to cluster functions by residual risk pattern. HYDRA requires no CVE labels at operation and targets latent zero-day risk in already-patched functions -- a detection problem for which supervised methods are ill-suited given the absence of ground-truth labels.

These approaches share the label-free objective with the present framework but each retains a task-specific component (ContraFlow's fine-tuned head, HYDRA's autoencoder), external infrastructure (IRIS's static analyser), or a curated CVE knowledge base (Vul-RAG).

\subsection{Positioning}
\label{sec:rw-gap}

Prior work in this space falls into four groups, each with a persistent constraint. Supervised VD requires CVE labels and generalises poorly to unseen projects \citep{chakraborty2021deep, risse2024uncovering}; direct-LLM VD inherits these limitations and adds prompt sensitivity; existing unsupervised approaches (\S\ref{sec:rw-unsupervised}) retain a task-specific training component, external static-analyser infrastructure, or a curated CVE knowledge base; and LLM-as-judge and generate-and-verify are established primitives that, to our knowledge, have not been combined into a security-focused anomaly-detection pipeline evaluated on paired CVE-fix data. The framework proposed here fills that gap: unsupervised anomaly detection over paired CVE-fix data using pretrained LLMs out of the box, requiring no CVE labels, no task-specific components, no external analyser, and no CVE knowledge base.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The pipeline follows four stages, summarised in
Figure~\ref{fig:framework}: a paired \emph{dataset} of vulnerable
and fixed C/C++ functions is constructed from a public vulnerability
corpus and validated case-by-case against the raw archive
(\S\ref{sec:methodology-data}); a \emph{reconstruction} stage draws five
independent implementations from each function's specification alone; a
\emph{judgement} stage rates one version of the real function at a time
against the consensus of those reconstructions
(\S\ref{sec:methodology-pipeline}--\S\ref{sec:methodology-judge}); and an
\emph{evaluation} stage aggregates the resulting scores into a pairwise
accuracy and a coverage figure (\S\ref{sec:methodology-metrics}). The
intended deployment role of the detector is as a filter ahead of a
downstream supervised classifier, so that expensive classification is
spent on functions already flagged as anomalous; this paper implements
and evaluates only the anomaly-detection stage itself, not the downstream
classifier.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:methodology-formulation}

Let $s$ denote a natural-language specification of a function's intended
behavior, and let $f$ denote a reference implementation nominally
satisfying $s$. Given $s$ alone, the detector draws $K$ independent
\emph{candidate} implementations $C = \{c_1,\dots,c_K\}$
(\S\ref{sec:methodology-pipeline}) and, comparing $f$ against the subset of
defensive behaviour on which a majority of $C$ agrees, produces an
\emph{exposure} score $e(C, f) \in [0,10]$: how much hostile-input exposure
the steps $f$ omits would leave. Higher scores indicate greater exposure.

Ground truth comes from paired CVE fixes: for each CVE, a vulnerable
implementation $f_{\text{vuln}}$ and its patched counterpart
$f_{\text{fixed}}$ both nominally satisfy the same specification $s$. The
detector is evaluated on the ordering it induces, and should therefore satisfy
\[
e(C, f_{\text{vuln}}) > e(C, f_{\text{fixed}}).
\]
The two scores are produced by independent judge invocations, neither of which
is told which version it holds, that a counterpart exists, or that a
vulnerability is involved.

\subsection{Dataset Construction and Validation}
\label{sec:methodology-data}

The evaluation corpus derives from the Big-Vul corpus \cite{fan2020ac},
filtered to CVE-fixing commits touching a single file. For each row, an
extraction step queried the GitHub API for the fixing commit's parent (the
vulnerable-side commit), downloaded the same file at both commits, and
re-derived changed-line ranges from the diff. A case-construction step then
matched each annotated function to its diff hunk by greedy maximum-overlap
matching on line ranges and extracted both the pre-patch and post-patch
function bodies. From 553 CVE-fixing commits this yields 792 function-level
cases, each one function in two versions with a structured specification
(Summary, Parameters, Returns, Logic).

\paragraph{Specification provenance.} Every specification starts from the
\textbf{original, pre-patch implementation}, describing the function as it
stood before the security fix landed. Claude Opus was then used to refine and
improve those docstrings from that same original code, producing the structured
four-field form used throughout. The provenance matters because it leaves a
measurable trace: identifiers unique to the pre-patch version appear in the
specifications $3.7\times$ more often than patched-only identifiers (sign test
$p = 2.9\times10^{-6}$). A faithful reconstruction from such a specification
should, if anything, resemble the vulnerable version, so the bias works
\emph{against} the detector. \S\ref{sec:eval-controls} quantifies it and the
Limitations return to it.

\paragraph{Validation.} Function extraction from patch line-ranges is
error-prone, and an error affecting one side of a pair biases a paired metric
directly. We validate every case against the raw archive before use, extracting
both sides through the same brace-matching code path: the snippet must be a
verbatim substring of the original file, both versions must be exactly one
complete function, the recorded line range must agree with the snippet, the two
versions must differ, and the specification must be present and non-trivial.
Some cases cannot be posed as a reconstruction task at all (a patched line at
file scope, a \texttt{\#define} macro with a statement-expression body, a
function renamed by the patch); we exclude these rather than repair them.
\textbf{626 cases} survive, 79\% of the 792. The corpus is content-addressed by
a \texttt{corpus\_sha} recorded on every result record, and validation gates
every run, so no result reported here can have come from an unvalidated corpus.

\paragraph{Composition.} The 626 usable cases comprise 595 C and 31 C++
functions drawn from 453 distinct CVEs. \texttt{torvalds/linux} contributes 337
of them, followed by ImageMagick, Chromium, FFmpeg, krb5 and radare2. With 54\%
of cases from a single repository, every interval reported in this paper
resamples whole CVEs rather than individual cases
(\S\ref{sec:methodology-metrics}). A further 87 cases reach the same function
through more than one CVE record; the pre-registered primary evaluation subset
excludes them, leaving \textbf{539 non-duplicate cases}, and we report the full
626 alongside as descriptive.

\subsection{Detection Pipeline}
\label{sec:methodology-pipeline}

\paragraph{Stage 1, reconstruction.} A generator model receives exactly
$\{\text{language}, \text{specification}\}$ and writes an implementation. It
never sees the real code, the CVE identifier, the repository, or the commit
message. We draw five independent samples at temperature 0.8.

\paragraph{Stage 2, judgement.} A judge model receives the specification, the
five reconstructions, and \emph{one} version of the real function, the
\emph{target}. It answers the question of \S\ref{sec:methodology-judge} about
majority-performed defensive steps and returns an exposure score on 0--10.

\paragraph{Stage 3, comparison.} The harness runs stage 2 twice per case, once
with the vulnerable version as target and once with the patched version. The
judge is never told which is which, and the two calls are independent: neither
sees the other's input or output. Whichever version received the higher
exposure score is the detector's answer.

\paragraph{Execution and reproducibility.} Both roles are filled by
Qwen3-32B-AWQ, served locally with vLLM on a single RTX 4090, under
grammar-constrained JSON decoding so that every response parses. Generator
temperature is 0.8 for the five samples, judge temperature 0.0, seed 1234. All
626 cases were judged at the full $K = 5$; none degraded on context length.
Every result record carries a digest of the exact rubric text
(\texttt{prompt\_sha}) and of the corpus (\texttt{corpus\_sha}), so any figure
in this paper traces to the prompt and the data that produced it. There are no
API costs: a full 626-case pass takes a few GPU-hours at $K = 5$ on one
consumer card.

\subsection{The Judge's Question}
\label{sec:methodology-judge}

The judge is asked a single, specific, non-lexical question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Which defensive steps does the reconstruction perform that the target
does not?}
\end{quote}

``Defensive step'' is enumerated concretely: a bounds or length check before
an access, a null or error-return check before use, an arithmetic overflow
guard, an initialisation or clearing before use, a lifetime step (free,
unlock, refcount, nulling after release), input validation, a permission
check, or an explicit bound on work or memory. The judge returns an exposure
score measuring how much hostile-input exposure the omitted steps leave, with
the range bound to severity classes: 0 for ``omits nothing'', 1--3 for ``hard
to reach'', 4--6 for ``a plausible issue on a reachable path'', and 7--10 for
``clear and serious''.

The rubric is equally explicit about what is \emph{not} a difference: naming,
types, helper functions, control-flow shape, error-handling style, formatting,
comments, and length. It also requires the same effect achieved differently
(\texttt{if (n > max) return -EINVAL} versus \texttt{n = MIN(n, max)}) to count
as the same step. \S\ref{sec:evolution} explains why closing off these escape
routes matters.

Consensus is imposed within the question itself. The judge is instructed to
ignore any step that only one reconstruction performs and to require at least a
majority before treating a step as expected. A defensive step taken by four or
five of the five reconstructions provides evidence that the step is implied by
the specification, or is ordinary practice for that kind of function, and we
treat it as specification-supported consensus; a step only one sample takes is
more plausibly sampling noise. Since all five samples come from the same model,
this is evidence about what that model reads the specification to require, not
an independent verification of it. The rubric additionally rates the
\emph{reverse} comparison, how exposed the reconstructions are by steps the
target performs and they omit, as a control. This yields the second decision
rule of \S\ref{sec:eval-operating}.

\subsection{The Three Constraints}
\label{sec:methodology-constraints}

Three properties keep the evaluation from being circular:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Information isolation.} The generator sees only
  $\{\text{language}, \text{specification}\}$, enforced structurally rather
  than by prompt: the request body is built from those two fields, so nothing
  else can reach it.
\item \textbf{One version at a time.} The judge compares against a single
  version and is never told which it is, that a counterpart exists, or that a
  vulnerability is involved.
\item \textbf{No supervised signal.} The vulnerable/patched pairing exists only
  in the evaluation harness. The detector never sees a label.
\end{enumerate}

We label any experiment that relaxes one of these a \emph{diagnostic} and never
report it as the method. \S\ref{sec:eval-diagnostic} is the only such result
here.

\subsection{A Worked Example}
\label{sec:methodology-example}

Consider a function whose specification says it copies a caller-supplied
buffer into a fixed-size destination.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Five reconstructions are drawn independently from that description. Four
  of the five write a length check before copying. Four of five is a majority,
  so the check is treated as specification-supported consensus rather than one
  sample's habit.
\item The judge is shown \textbf{version A} and asked what it omits relative to
  that consensus. It reports: no bound on the copy length, reachable with an
  oversized input. Exposure score 6.
\item The judge is shown \textbf{version B}, in a separate call with no
  knowledge of the first. It reports that the length check is performed.
  Exposure score 0.
\item The harness concludes that version A is the vulnerable one. It happens to
  be right, but nothing in steps 1--3 knew that.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:methodology-metrics}

The evaluation corpus is pair-balanced by construction: every case supplies
exactly one vulnerable and one fixed version of the same function. We define
the metric jointly over both sides of a case, so it does not depend on an
assumed base rate.

\textbf{Accuracy} is the fraction of \emph{decided} cases the detector ranked
correctly:
\[
\text{accuracy} = \frac{|\{e(C,f_{\text{vuln}}) > e(C,f_{\text{fixed}})\}|}
                      {|\{e(C,f_{\text{vuln}}) \neq e(C,f_{\text{fixed}})\}|}.
\]
\textbf{Chance is exactly 50\%.} A detector with no information, forced to
choose one of two versions, is right half the time. There is no class-imbalance
correction to argue about: every case has exactly one vulnerable and one
patched version, so the baseline is 50\% by construction and cannot be moved by
how often the detector chooses to flag anything.

\textbf{Coverage} is the fraction of cases that are decided, meaning those
whose two scores differ. Cases scoring equal on both sides are
\emph{undecided}. We report them as coverage and never count them as successes
or failures, since scoring them either way would let the reported accuracy be
manufactured at will. Accuracy is thus conditional on answering, and we report
the two figures together throughout. \S\ref{sec:eval-coverage} accounts for
where the coverage goes and \S\ref{sec:eval-operating} gives a second operating
point that trades accuracy for it.

\textbf{Interval estimation.} One CVE commit often patches several functions,
and those cases succeed or fail together, so case-level independence does not
hold and case-resampled intervals would understate uncertainty. We therefore
resample whole CVEs: all intervals here are CVE-clustered bootstraps over
10{,}000 resamples, taking the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. This is stricter
than the textbook binomial interval, which is why the intervals below are wider
than a naive calculation would give. Reported $p$-values are exact two-sided
binomial tests against 50\%.

An earlier formulation of this work was pre-registered on a different metric,
\emph{Paired Flag Accuracy}, whose chance level is 25\% rather than 50\%. We
define and use it only in the Appendix, never alongside a 50\%-chance figure.


\section{How the Method Arrived at This Question}
\label{sec:evolution}

The method of \S\ref{sec:methodology} is our third formulation of this idea. We
report the first two because the reasons they failed determined the design, and
because one of those reasons generalises well beyond this work. The Appendix
records their numerical results.

\paragraph{The natural formulation carries no signal.} Our first implementation
generates one reconstruction from the specification, shows the judge that
reconstruction alongside one version of the real function, and asks it to rate
behavioural similarity and assign a category from a five-way taxonomy
(equivalent, quality bug, functional mismatch, security concern, degenerate).
Whichever real version is judged more divergent is called the vulnerable one.
It sits at chance. Three explanations were available: a generator too weak, a
generator given too little context, or a reconstruction too unfaithful. We
tested each rather than choosing between them. A stronger generator model made
no difference, and a wider context ablation was negative.

\paragraph{The judge was comparing text, not behaviour.} The experiment that
settled the question replaced the generated reconstruction with a
\emph{perfect} one, handing the judge the genuine patched function as the
candidate. We then degraded that candidate only in ways the rubric declares
irrelevant, renaming local variables and stripping comments, which leaves
behaviour, control flow and semantics untouched. The rubric states in as many
words that different variable names, type names, helper names and code style
are \emph{not} behavioural differences. Renaming locals anyway cost roughly a
quarter of the metric and a fifth of the ROC-AUC. An internal control within
that arm confirms the mechanism: cases with no renameable local variables
scored several times higher than cases with five or more renames.

We draw two conclusions from this, and together they set the subsequent
direction. An LLM judge's apparent ability to compare two implementations
behaviourally is largely lexical, because a broad ``how similar are these?''
question invites it to measure surface overlap, which is what it can actually
measure. We regard this as the most transferable finding in the paper, since it
constrains LLM-as-judge code comparison in general. Separately, the perfect
reconstruction scored no better than the generated one, so reconstruction
\emph{fidelity} was not the binding constraint, matching what the two generator
experiments above had already found.

If the candidate is not the problem, the question is. An independent
reconstruction shares almost no identifiers with the original by construction,
placing it at the far end of that degradation curve, so a broad similarity
question is poorly suited to this setting. This motivates the narrow,
enumerated predicate of \S\ref{sec:methodology-judge} and its explicit
not-a-difference list.

\paragraph{One reconstruction rarely contains the guard.} Narrowing the
question alone was still at chance, and the failure left a visible trace. With
a single reconstruction the judge rated the real function's exposure at zero on
roughly three cases in five on \emph{both} sides, leaving the pair undecidable,
and the mean exposure difference pointed marginally the wrong way. The cause is
structural. A from-specification reconstruction written by an LLM is less
defensive than mature production code, so comparing real kernel code against
one such sample usually finds nothing the sample does that the real code does
not. One sample also cannot distinguish its own arbitrary choices from the
specification's requirements, since any single draw from a stochastic generator
contains both.

Consensus across five reconstructions addresses both problems. The union of
five is more likely to contain the guard the vulnerable version lacks than any
one of them, while the majority requirement keeps that union from collapsing
into a list of five samples' idiosyncrasies. No label is involved at any point,
which is what keeps the method unsupervised.

Neither change suffices alone. The narrow question without consensus is at
chance, and consensus was worth trying only because the oracle experiment had
pointed at the judge's question rather than at reconstruction fidelity, and the
single-sample diagnosis had shown that one sample could not answer it. We
therefore attribute the result below to the two changes jointly.
\S\ref{sec:eval-main} does not separate them, and the Limitations name the
ablation that would.

\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Main Result}
\label{sec:eval-main}

case, the judge shown one version at a time, and all three constraints of
\S\ref{sec:methodology-constraints} intact.

Of the 205 pairs the method was willing to rank, it placed the vulnerable

lower end. No supervised signal enters this figure: the generator saw a
specification and nothing else, the judge saw one function at a time without
being told what it was looking at, and the vulnerable/patched labels exist only
in the scoring harness.

\subsection{Where the Coverage Goes}
\label{sec:eval-coverage}

Since accuracy is conditional on answering, the 38\% coverage figure needs an
account, and much of it appears to be mechanical. The rubric binds score ranges
to severity classes (\S\ref{sec:methodology-judge}), and the judge answers at
the level of the class rather than within it, so the large majority of scores
take one of three values. Two versions of the same function usually fall in the
same class, and when they do their scores are identical and the case goes
undecided. Only a small minority of ties are the benign case where both sides
score zero; the rest are two functions the judge placed in the same band.

Much of the coverage loss thus appears to arise from the rubric's coarse
quantisation rather than from an absence of signal, which makes a score rated
independently of the severity class the most direct candidate for raising
coverage without disturbing the question. We have not established that
quantisation accounts for all of it. Mean exposure is nonetheless higher on
vulnerable versions than on patched ones across the whole corpus, before any
thresholding, which fits the same reading.

\subsection{A Second Operating Point}
\label{sec:eval-operating}

The decision rule of \S\ref{sec:methodology-pipeline} uses the target's own
exposure. The rubric also records the reverse comparison introduced in
\S\ref{sec:methodology-judge}, and differencing the two removes an offset
common to both judge calls, since a weak reconstruction makes everything
compared against it look defensively better. This second rule decides
substantially more cases at a moderately lower accuracy while remaining well
clear of chance, so the method is tunable along a coverage--accuracy frontier
rather than fixed at one operating point. The Appendix tabulates both.

Thresholding on the score margin, by contrast, buys nothing. The quantisation
described above leaves the margin carrying little information beyond its sign,
so demanding a larger margin does not improve accuracy and no confident subset
can be extracted. The useful knob is the score definition, not the threshold.

\subsection{Controls}
\label{sec:eval-controls}

\paragraph{Length.} Security patches usually \emph{add} lines, so ``the shorter
version is the vulnerable one'' is a rule requiring no intelligence at all, and
on this corpus it is right on the large majority of unequal pairs. To check
whether the method is doing anything beyond that, we restrict it to pairs whose
two versions have the same line count, where the shortcut carries no
information. \textbf{On those pairs the method scores 68.5\%} (95\% CI
[56.4\%, 80.0\%], excluding chance), slightly above its overall accuracy rather
than below it. A detector covertly ranking by line count could not produce that
result, and across the corpus the method favours the shorter side no more often
than its accuracy alone implies. The Appendix gives per-stratum figures.

Constraint \#2 (\S\ref{sec:methodology-constraints}) thus acts as a defence and
not only a cost, since a formulation that never sees the two versions together
cannot exploit a shortcut living in the comparison between them. We suggest
paired fix-pair evaluations report the equal-length stratum for this reason.

\paragraph{Specification leakage.} A small number of cases carry a heuristic
flag for specification text that may describe the fix, and a prior audit had
already removed specifications that stated the fix outright. Excluding the
flagged cases leaves accuracy unchanged (Appendix).

\paragraph{Specification provenance.} As established in
\S\ref{sec:methodology-data}, each specification starts from the original,
pre-patch implementation and was refined by Claude Opus from that same original
code. Identifiers unique to the pre-patch side accordingly appear in the
specifications $3.7\times$ more often than patched-only identifiers ($p =
2.9\times10^{-6}$). The direction of this bias is against the detector: a
faithful reconstruction from such a specification should resemble the
vulnerable version, which makes the vulnerable side \emph{harder}, not easier,
to flag. If anything, the reported accuracy is conservative.

\paragraph{Structural controls.} Information isolation is enforced by
construction rather than by prompt, since the generator's request body is built
from two fields and code or CVE metadata cannot reach it. The judge rubric is
digest-pinned (\texttt{prompt\_sha}), so no arm can silently drift from the
text it claims to use. The two judge calls per case are independent, neither
conditioned on the other's input or verdict, and the corpus is
content-addressed and validation-gated (\S\ref{sec:methodology-data}).

\subsection{Diagnostic: the Narrow Question with Both Versions}
\label{sec:eval-diagnostic}

This diagnostic relaxes constraint \#2. The judge is shown both real versions
at once, in randomised order, and asked which is missing a guard, with
\emph{neither} permitted and scored as an abstention so that accuracy cannot be
inflated by forcing guesses. It decides 85.1\% of cases at \textbf{82.4\%}
accuracy (95\% CI [78.6\%, 85.9\%]).

Removing the reconstruction entirely isolates the question from it, and the
result shows that the narrow predicate carries strong signal where the broad
similarity question of \S\ref{sec:evolution}, given the same access, carried
none. It is not a deployable detector. In the field one has a single function
rather than a matched pair, and having the pair would make detection
unnecessary.

Nor should the figure be read as a bound on what is knowable in principle,
since a meaningful share of it is surface. Under the length stratification of
\S\ref{sec:eval-controls}, this arm is strongest where line count points the
right way and falls below chance where it points the wrong way. Showing the
judge both texts at once hands it the comparison the shortcut lives in, and it
takes it, which is a further instance of the lexical dependence diagnosed in
\S\ref{sec:evolution}. We report the arm as a diagnostic rather than a ceiling
for this reason.

\subsection{It Is Not Simply ``Ask the Model''}
\label{sec:eval-baselines}

Asked directly whether a single function is vulnerable, the same model flags
only about a tenth of vulnerable sides and lands at chance on AUC, below even
the superseded first formulation. Supplying the specification alongside the
function barely helps. A conventional static analyser (\texttt{flawfinder}
2.0.20) does worse still on these snippets, though partly because they are bare
functions presented without headers, macros or types. The Appendix gives
figures for all three.

What separates the method from these baselines is not access to the function,
which they all have, but how the comparison is posed.


\section*{Limitations and Future Directions}

\paragraph{Coverage.} The method answers on 205 of 539 cases, and reported
accuracy is conditional on answering (\S\ref{sec:methodology-metrics}).
\S\ref{sec:eval-coverage} accounts for much of it and
\S\ref{sec:eval-operating} offers a higher-coverage operating point, but a
deployment would still need to know whether the abstention region is benign or
systematically conceals a vulnerability class. We have not characterised it.
The most promising remedy is a severity score rated independently of the
severity class, which we expect would raise coverage without changing the
question; it is the highest-leverage remaining change to the method itself.

\paragraph{The anti-length stratum is underpowered.} Pairs whose vulnerable
side is the longer one form the smallest of the length strata, with too few
decided cases to support a conclusion; its interval is consistent with the
equal-length result and with chance alike (Appendix).

\paragraph{The two changes are not separated.} We attribute the gain jointly to
the narrow question and to five-sample consensus. The intermediate arm, the
narrow question with a single reconstruction, was measured before the
validation pass of \S\ref{sec:methodology-data} and has not been re-run on the
final corpus, so the split between the two changes is not isolated on identical
data. A $K = 1/3/5$ ablation would resolve this cheaply, reusing the candidate
pool already on disk so that only the judge re-runs; we expect accuracy to
increase monotonically in $K$, with the largest step between 1 and 3. Pushing
$K$ further and varying the sampling temperature are the natural follow-ons,
since $K = 5$ and temperature 0.8 were both chosen without tuning.

\paragraph{One model family.} Qwen3-32B fills both roles, so generator and
judge share pretraining and the design does not exclude correlation arising
from that. Crossing them across model families would test the confound directly
and show whether the result belongs to the formulation or to this model.
Agreement across independent architectures would also support the reading that
the detected divergence reflects program behaviour rather than a model-specific
artefact. Robustness should in general be established across seeds, prompts and
architectures rather than inferred from one configuration.

\paragraph{One dataset, dominated by one repository.} 54\% of cases are Linux
kernel code. CVE clustering corrects the variance estimate, not the
composition.

\paragraph{Specification provenance.} Specifications start from the pre-patch
code and were refined by Claude Opus from that same code
(\S\ref{sec:methodology-data}). \S\ref{sec:eval-controls} argues that this
biases against the method, but the causal test is unrun: regenerating
specifications from the \emph{patched} side and re-running would show whether
the provenance sets the direction of the detector's bias. The issue is not
confined to this dataset. A project's documentation is written alongside
whatever version of the code currently exists, which is the vulnerable one
until a patch lands, so any specification-reconstruction detector inherits the
bias wherever documentation is not authored independently of the
implementation.

\paragraph{Scope.} C and C++, predominantly memory-safety and input-validation
CVEs. Nothing here speaks to logic flaws, race conditions, or managed-language
vulnerabilities.

\paragraph{Ties are excluded, not resolved.} Reporting accuracy on the decided
subset is the honest option available, but it leaves the method's behaviour on
the remaining 62\% of cases described only by its coverage.

\paragraph{Supervised comparison as the next evaluation stage.} We did not
proceed to a full comparison with supervised detectors such as VulDeePecker,
LineVul and VulBERTa, since the study first had to establish whether
reconstruction-based behavioural divergence provides a meaningful signal on its
own. Now that it does, evaluating it alongside those detectors is the natural
next step. The two approaches rely on different signals, supervised models
learning vulnerability patterns from labelled historical examples while this
method reasons about divergence from independently reconstructed intent, so
their errors may be complementary rather than redundant.

\paragraph{Testing on genuinely unseen CVEs.} A temporal evaluation, training
supervised classifiers only on vulnerabilities disclosed before a fixed cutoff
and testing on CVEs disclosed after it, would exercise the use case that
motivates this work. The disagreements would be the informative cases. Where
both paradigms agree, the result is convergent evidence from two independent
detection mechanisms. Where the behavioural detector raises an anomaly and the
supervised classifier predicts benign, the case warrants deeper analysis rather
than dismissal as an ordinary false positive, since manual investigation could
determine whether the disagreement reflects benign variation, a modelling
error, or a vulnerability pattern absent from the labelled training data. This
is where reconstruct-and-diff is most likely to add something a supervised
classifier cannot.


\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We set out to test whether a vulnerability can be found without labels,
without training, and without any prior description of the vulnerability, using
only the function's own documentation. How much of that is achievable turns
largely on how the comparison is put to the language model.

The natural formulation, regenerating the function from its specification and
asking a judge how similar the regeneration is to the real code, carries no
signal at all. Our diagnosis is that a broad comparison of two implementations
is answered substantially lexically, while a specification-only reconstruction
shares almost no surface with the code it reconstructs, making a
lexically-answerable question a poor fit for this setting. The redesigned
formulation puts a narrow, enumerated question about omitted defensive steps to
the judge and asks it about the consensus of five independent reconstructions
rather than one. It reaches 66.8\% pairwise accuracy on 539 validated CVE
fix-pairs against a 50\% chance level, on the 38\% of pairs it decides, with no
supervised signal at any stage, and holds up on the pairs where the obvious
line-count shortcut is unavailable.

Our experiments indicate that the judge formulation, rather than reconstruction
fidelity, was the dominant constraint: a perfect reconstruction scored no
better than a generated one, while changing what the judge was asked moved the
result substantially. We expect the underlying lesson to transfer beyond this
task. LLM-as-judge designs that compare two artefacts should be tested against
surface-preserving perturbations before their comparisons are believed to be
semantic, and paired CVE-fix evaluations should report the equal-length stratum
before their results are believed to be about security.

\subsection*{Supporting figures for the final method}

The figures below support claims the body makes qualitatively. All use the
pairwise metric of \S\ref{sec:methodology-metrics}, chance 50\%.

\emph{Operating points} (\S\ref{sec:eval-operating}), on the 626-case
descriptive set: the target-exposure rule of \S\ref{sec:methodology-pipeline}
decides 37.7\% of cases at 65.3\% accuracy (95\% CI [59.1\%, 71.4\%]); the
net-exposure rule decides 55.0\% at 59.0\% (95\% CI [53.8\%, 64.2\%]). Both
exclude chance: 17 points of coverage cost 6 points of accuracy.

\emph{Thresholding} (\S\ref{sec:eval-operating}): requiring a minimum score
margin of 1, 2, 3 or 4 gives accuracies of 65.3\%, 67.6\%, 63.4\% and 67.2\% on
236, 188, 82 and 61 decided cases respectively. There is no confident subset to
extract.

\emph{Length strata} (\S\ref{sec:eval-controls}): the vulnerable side is
shorter in 415 of 626 pairs, equal in 148 and longer in 63, so a bare
line-count rule is right on 86.8\% of the pairs where it applies. The method
scores 66.9\% on the 154 decided pairs where the vulnerable side is shorter,
68.5\% on the 54 equal-length ones, and 50.0\% on the 28 where it is longer;
the last of these is too small to interpret. Across unequal decided pairs the
method chooses the shorter side 64.3\% of the time, close to the 60.4\% a
length-blind detector of this accuracy would produce by chance. The
both-versions diagnostic of \S\ref{sec:eval-diagnostic} behaves very
differently on the same strata, scoring 92.0\%, 77.0\% and 26.8\% respectively
and choosing the shorter side on 89.6\% of unequal pairs, which is why we
report it as a diagnostic rather than a ceiling.

\emph{Specification leakage} (\S\ref{sec:eval-controls}): 32 of the 626 cases
carry the heuristic flag. Accuracy on the 594 unflagged cases is 64.9\%,
against 65.3\% on the full descriptive set.

\emph{Score distribution} (\S\ref{sec:eval-coverage}): 92\% of all exposure
scores are exactly 0, 4 or 6; 62.3\% of cases tie, and 14.4\% of those ties are
the ``both clean'' case where both sides score 0. Mean exposure is 3.93 on
vulnerable versions against 3.63 on patched ones.
